Comedy as Folding

The smile, the pun and the joke: a message that unfolds in the listener, and the laugh as the moment it lands.

Marin Kokona · Working notes · October 2026 · Nothing here is claimed as proved

Abstract

A joke is a fold: it is compressed when it is told, it unfolds in the listener, and the laugh is the moment it lands. That is why comedy carries a message better than a speech does: the listener does the unfolding, and the unfolding is a pleasure. I follow the move through its forms in order of length: the smile, which needs no words; the pun, one word carrying two meanings, with this site's own address as a found example; and the joke, with Ricky Gervais as the credit for a cut aimed at the frame rather than the person. A risk runs alongside: a laugh can be an exit, release without the point. The account sits inside the old incongruity tradition and adds only the link to Folding. It is a way of looking, not a theory of humour.

Keywords: comedy · folding · pun · incongruity · self-reading address · Ricky Gervais

1Comedy is folding

Folding [1] followed one move through metabolism, algorithms and physics: compress a structure into a seed, keep what matters, and let it unfold again. Watching the Split [2] kept reaching for one example of that move without stopping to look at it: the joke. This page stops to look.

The core idea

A joke is a fold. It is compressed when it is told and it unfolds in the listener, and the laugh is the moment it lands.
That is why comedy is such a good way to deliver a message. The unfolding happens in a positive way: it makes people laugh. A politician's speech on a subject is boring. The same message from a comedian is interesting, and funny.
Think of juice and fibre. The politician hands over the conclusion already unfolded, like juice: easy to swallow and gone at once. The comedian hands over the fold, and the listener discovers the point themselves. The work of unfolding is what makes it stay.
This is also why a joke cannot be explained. Explaining it unfolds it for you, and leaves nothing to land. The listener has to perform the last fold.

A risk to watch

A laugh can also be an exit: the release arrives and the point is dropped. You laugh, and nothing has changed. A good comedian guards against this by leaving a sting, something that keeps unfolding after the laugh has passed. Section 5 comes back to this.

2The shortest forms

If a joke is a fold, it is worth asking how tight a fold can be and still land. The pun is the tightest fold that uses words: one word carrying two meanings at once. There may be something shorter still, the smile, which needs no words at all.
That gives a possible order, from the most folded to the least, and it is the order of the next three sections.

3The smile

In situations that have become overly serious or critical, I start smiling. It is not resignation. I am smiling at how funny it is when a small argument gets tangled up in seriousness: a small argument wearing a serious costume.
The gap between the costume and the thing wearing it is the joke. It is the same move as a pun: one thing, two readings. The situation reads as grave, and it also reads as small, and the smile is the moment both readings are held at once.
This is the oldest account of humour, incongruity (Section 7), arrived at from the inside. It does not always work. A smile in a serious room can be read as not taking the room seriously. But when it does work, it does what a joke does without saying anything: it lets the people in the room see the gap for themselves.

4The pun: a self-reading address

Much of what I write is about addresses: the fold as an address into a space of possibilities, alternative addresses, and the search for the right one, when in fact the address was in hand all along. Then I found a pun that does exactly this.

4.1The address that says what it is

The usual way to find someone online is a .com address. With the newer top-level domains, I asked myself what my own website would be, and the answer was the address of this site:
marin.kokona.website, which reads out as a sentence: Marin Kokona, website. The address says what it is. It is a self-reading address.
And I read my own address: I read my own website. A website address is better than a home address in one way that matters here. Anyone can visit it and read it.

4.2Found, not prepared

To be honest about it: I did not build this pun. I became aware of it after I had already lived in it. That difference matters. A built pun feels clever; a found pun feels true. The built one shows the skill of the person folding. The found one shows that the fold was already there, waiting to be read.

Why the address has outside readers

A self-reading loop is safe only when it is not sealed. A thermostat reads its own temperature and corrects itself, but it still needs batteries from outside to keep running. A website that only its owner reads is a sealed loop. Outside readers are the batteries.

5The joke, and Ricky Gervais

Ricky Gervais is my favourite comedian. For a long time it was Chris Rock; Gervais came later. He is controversial and he cuts deep, and for a long time I did not know why I liked him.

5.1The first sign

The first sign was his Q&A at the Oxford Union [3]. Talking about Karl Pilkington, he said that one of the most amazing facts he knows is that there are as many even numbers as even and odd numbers put together (at 27:51). Karl would not have it. It is a real result from the mathematics of infinity: an infinite set can be paired off, one to one, with a part of itself [4]. My reaction was simple. He knows about that too.

5.2After that

After that I started to hear the structure under the jokes. One line of his, which he repeats often and says in that same talk (at 3:14), sums it up:
“Just because you're offended doesn't mean you're right.”
A feeling cannot be what settles an argument. That is the same guardrail as the real enemy in Watching the Split: any frame arranged so that nothing could ever show it wrong. Offence that ends the discussion is one of those frames.

5.3The tie to folding

What the joke does

A joke takes what everyone treats as settled and reveals the hidden dimension in it. In Folding, paradoxes dissolve when the missing dimension is added back. In comedy, the missing dimension is added back in front of an audience, and the laugh is the sound of it landing.
What makes Gervais a credit here, and not only a favourite, is where he aims. He attacks the problem, not the people, and he holds an awareness of not taking the humour too far. The cut is aimed at the frame, not the person.
He also answers the risk from Section 1. His jokes rarely end at the laugh. They leave a sting, and the sting is what keeps the fold unfolding after the room has gone quiet. The laugh is not the exit; it is the door.

6An example: a loop seen from inside

Spoiler

This section gives away the fate of a character in The Counselor (2013).
In Ridley Scott's The Counselor [5], Westray, played by Brad Pitt, explains early on a device called the bolito: a wire loop that, once fastened, closes on its own and cannot be stopped. Late in the film, Westray is killed with exactly that device. The loop he described closes on him.
In that moment there is something close to a smile on his face. The film leaves it open. My reading is that it is not resignation. He is laughing at the irony.
“I did this to myself.” There is some comedy in that. It is the same gap I smile at in a serious room: a loop seen from inside, and seen, for one moment, as funny.
That puts the scene on the comedy side. It is an example, not the lead: what it shows is that the recognition a joke produces does not need a comedian. It can arrive on its own, even at the worst possible time, whenever someone sees the fold they are standing in.

7Related work, and where this stops

The idea that a joke is a gap between two readings is old. Kant described laughter as an expectation that suddenly comes to nothing [6], and Schopenhauer located the comic in the incongruity between a concept and the real thing it is applied to [7]. This is the incongruity tradition, and the smile of Section 3 sits squarely inside it.
Koestler called the same move bisociation: one situation perceived in two frames of reference at once, each consistent on its own [9]. It is the closest earlier statement of “one thing, two readings”, and he too saw it running through humour, science and art alike. Freud's account of the joke as a release of held tension [8] is the other side: it explains why a laugh can be an exit, the risk named in Section 1. Hurley, Dennett and Adams treat humour as the reward for catching a hidden mistake in one's own reasoning [10], which is close to adding back a missing dimension.
What this page adds is only the link: that these are the same move as the fold in Folding, the compression that unfolds in a reader. It is not a theory of humour and it does not explain why some folds land and others do not. Like the papers before it, it is a way of looking, and nothing here is claimed as proved.
