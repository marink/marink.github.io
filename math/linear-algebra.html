Linear Algebra

Linear algebra is the study of vectors, matrices, and linear transformations. It is the language of machine learning, computer graphics, physics, and data science — arguably the most practically important branch of mathematics.

Vectors

A vector in Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn is an ordered list of nnn real numbers, written as a column:

v=(v1v2⋮vn)\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix}v=​v1​v2​⋮vn​​​

Matrices

Definition — Matrix

An m×nm \times nm×n matrix is a rectangular array of numbers with mmm rows and nnn columns. The entry in row iii, column jjj is denoted AijA_{ij}Aij​.
A=(123456)(2\times3 matrix)A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{(2\times 3 matrix)}A=(14​25​36​)(2\times3 matrix)

A matrix represents a linear transformation: multiplying by AAA maps vectors from Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn to Rm\mathbb{R}^mRm. Every linear transformation between finite-dimensional spaces can be represented as a matrix.

Matrix Operations

Multiplication

The product ABABAB of an m×nm\times nm×n matrix AAA and an n×pn\times pn×p matrix BBB is the m×pm\times pm×p matrix with entries:

(AB)ij=∑k=1nAikBkj(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n A_{ik} B_{kj}(AB)ij​=k=1∑n​Aik​Bkj​

Transpose

The transpose ATA^TAT is obtained by reflecting over the diagonal — rows become columns:

(AT)ij=Aji(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}(AT)ij​=Aji​

Inverse

A square matrix AAA is invertible if there exists A−1A^{-1}A−1 such that AA−1=A−1A=IA A^{-1} = A^{-1} A = IAA−1=A−1A=I, where III is the identity matrix. AAA is invertible if and only if det⁡(A)≠0\det(A) \neq 0det(A)=0.

Linear Systems

A system of linear equations can be written compactly as Ax=bA\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}Ax=b:

(2153)(xy)=(47)\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\5 & 3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}4\\7\end{pmatrix}(25​13​)(xy​)=(47​)

If AAA is invertible, the unique solution is x=A−1b\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}x=A−1b. In practice, Gaussian elimination — row-reducing the augmented matrix [A∣b][A \mid \mathbf{b}][A∣b] — is more efficient. A system has either zero, one, or infinitely many solutions.

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Definition — Eigenvalue / Eigenvector

A nonzero vector v\mathbf{v}v is an eigenvector of matrix AAA with eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ if:
Av=λvA\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}Av=λv
Geometrically: AAA stretches (or reflects) v\mathbf{v}v by the scalar factor λ\lambdaλ, leaving its direction unchanged.

Eigenvalues are found by solving the characteristic equation:

det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A - \lambda I) = 0det(A−λI)=0

For each eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, the corresponding eigenvectors are the nonzero solutions to (A−λI)v=0(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}(A−λI)v=0.

Example: For A=(3102)A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}A=(30​12​), the characteristic equation is (3−λ)(2−λ)=0(3-\lambda)(2-\lambda)=0(3−λ)(2−λ)=0, giving eigenvalues λ1=3\lambda_1 = 3λ1​=3, λ2=2\lambda_2 = 2λ2​=2.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are fundamental to principal component analysis (PCA), Google's PageRank algorithm, quantum mechanics, and the analysis of differential equations.
